Three External Forces Impacting SEL

Social-Emotional Learning does not exist in a vacuum. Three major external forces — political, financial, and public health — are actively shaping how the industry grows, contracts, and responds.

01
External Threat

The Critical Race Theory and — SEL as a Political Target

Political Headwind

Social Emotional Learning is becoming one of the most talked about topics when it comes to Critical Race Theory. All 50 states have standards related to SEL in preschool and more than half have standards that follow through K-12, just under different names: character education, 21st century skills, noncognitive skills, and even soft skills (NPR). Conservatives have recently begun linking SEL to CRT, starting in Virginia Beach. Critical Race Theory is a concept about systemic racism being entrenched in our laws, politics, and institutions (Anderson, 2022). Starting around 2020, there has been an increase in protests about the inclusion of CRT in education. Conservative politicians have recently begun using Social-Emotional Learning as a connection to CRT, calling it the “Trojan horse” for CRT and transgender advocacy (Anderson, 2022). This has had real impacts in schools.

Tying Critical Race Theory to Social-Emotional Learning may harm students' health and well-being. This connection makes the Social-Emotional Learning industry vulnerable to changes in funding, research support, and more (“Unfounded Outrage over Critical Race Theory Risks Social Emotional Learning,” n.d.).

Key Evidence
  • 19+ states have introduced or passed legislation restricting or scrutinizing SEL programs as of 2024
  • “In Georgia, an administrator that was tasked with leading the district’s diversity, equity, and inclusion efforts was forced to resign before even starting work when protestors began pushing social-emotional learning as synonymous to critical race theory” (Anderson, 2022).
  • “In Wisconsin, Republican lawmakers introduced a bill trying to limit how educators can talk about race and racism in the classroom. One lawmaker added an addendum of terms that he thought were associated with critical race theory, including social-emotional learning” (Anderson, 2022).
⚠️ Industry Implication: This trend creates a two-tiered market where SEL adoption is increasingly determined by political geography rather than evidence. Companies operating nationally must navigate drastically different regulatory environments state by state, and content that is welcome in one district may be banned in the next.
02
External Force

Department of Education Funding — Change of Administration

Policy & Funding Pressure

The American Rescue Plan Act (2021) provided a $122 billion historic federal investment to help states and districts reopen schools safely while addressing students’ academic, social, and emotional learning after COVID-19 (Godek et al., n.d.). The recommendation of the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning was to use part of the funding to promote SEL in schools, support capacity building, and align SEL efforts across schools, families, and communities (Godek et al., n.d.). There were some highlights in usage of this funding as many schools distributed funds to initiatives that supported Social-Emotional Learning. However, this didn’t last long. With the change in political administration, the Department of Education has seen detrimental changes. Recent actions taken by President Donald Trump and the administration have reinvigorated political pushback to social-emotional learning (Vilcarino & Prothero, 2025). In early pandemic days, many political arguments claimed that Social-Emotional Learning was a way to inject progressive ideology into classrooms, which has led to the Department of Education saying Social-Emotional Learning is a means of discrimination (White House Blocks $2 Billion for Education: See All the Affected Programs, n.d.). Much of this scrutiny stems from people not really knowing what Social-Emotional Learning entails and how to define it. In addition to the cynicism surrounding SEL, the Department of Education is seeing dramatic funding cuts that are impacting schools nationwide. The Trump administration has made aggressive steps to shut down the entire Department of Education and move it to the Department of Health and Human Services and Department of Justice. This shift has elicited outcry from teachers, education advocates, and more. The Administration made this move under the guise of “moving education back to the states where it belongs,” however many are concerned with how this will impact student health, well-being, and students with disabilities (Changes at Education Department Will Leave Many Vulnerable Students at Risk | Truthout, n.d.). These changes will have a significant impact on the Social-Emotional Learning industry. It is clear that no department is safe under the current political landscape, and if scrutiny is already present then the industry is already in danger. The changes in the Department of Education worry many that student health and well-being are not taken seriously.

Key Evidence
  • The American Rescue Plan Act (2021) injected $122 billion into K-12 education, fueling the SEL market surge to $4.0B by 2024
  • Political shift have rehomed the Department of Education and leave student wellness at risk.
📊 Industry Implication: Federal funding is both the industry's greatest opportunity and its greatest vulnerability. The companies best positioned to weather funding shifts are those with free or low-cost core offerings — like GoNoodle — that do not depend on district budget cycles to maintain adoption.
03
External Driver

The Youth Mental Health Crisis — The Demand That Won't Go Away

Public Health Urgency

There is a lingering shadow of COVID-19 that has been impacting children in our communities. Before COVID-19, suicide rates amongst adolescents 15 to 19 increase to 8 per 100,000 in 2000 to 11.8 per 100,000 in 2017 (Miron et al., 2019). This was a significant escalation, however trends seen post-pandemic raise concerns that youth are in crisis. In the United States, studies show that more than 37% of high school students are experiencing poor mental health (Coronavirus Disease 2019, 2024). Mental health concerns were rising before the pandemic, and since there has been an escalation in these same concerns. There is a youth mental health crisis that would greatly benefit from including Social-Emotional Learning initiatives in schools. These concerns are immediate, with suicide being the leading cause of death in older adolescents (Mental Health of Adolescents, n.d.). Including curriculum like Social-Emotional Learning, which teaches students how to regulate emotions, reduce anxiety, and build emotional-intelligence, can help integrate protective factors into students’ routines and help buffer mental health risks (“SEL and Mental Health,” n.d.).

Key Evidence
  • Youth suicide rates rose 85.3% between 2007 and 2017, representing the foundational public health crisis driving SEL demand
  • More than 37% of high school students reported experiencing poor mental health
  • SEL is proven to build protective factors into student routines and help buffer mental health crises
🌟 Industry Implication: The youth mental health crisis is the most durable demand driver in the SEL industry. It ensures that even as political headwinds and funding fluctuations create market volatility, the underlying need for SEL tools will not disappear — making this an industry with long-term structural demand regardless of short-term conditions.

How These Three Forces Interact

These three external forces do not operate independently — they are actively in tension with each other, creating a complex environment for the SEL industry to navigate. The youth mental health crisis creates undeniable demand; federal funding has historically created the financial infrastructure to meet that demand; and political resistance around CRT has created a counterforce that threatens both the funding and the cultural permission for schools to act on it.

The result is an industry that is simultaneously growing and under threat. Companies that understand this three-way tension — and can position themselves as evidence-based, politically neutral, and financially accessible — are best positioned to thrive regardless of how the political and funding landscape shifts. GoNoodle's free, movement-based model, grounded in peer-reviewed research, reflects exactly this kind of positioning.
