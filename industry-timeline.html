Ancient Origins

The Whole Child Always Mattered

The roots of Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) are sewn deep into Ancient Greece, written in Plato's ideas about education in The Republic (Edutopa). In his work, Plato touches on a "holistic curriculum that requires balance of training" in all areas, such as physical movement, arts, math, science, character, and moral judgment (Edutopia). Plato was convinced that a thorough and balanced education was how a society would produce "citizens of good character" (Edutopia).

1920s–1930s

Foundational Frameworks

Influenced by Plato’s The Republic, Lev Vygotsky emphasized the key role that social interaction plays in the development of cognition. Vygotsky’s work in sociocultural theory suggested that the ways in which people interact with others and the culture they live in shapes their mental abilities (Fresno State University). His work on Zone of Proximal Development was critical in developing the frameworks for social-emotional learning. In this theory, Vygotsky argues that learning should be related to the child’s developmental level and their potential learning ability; this approach relates to more than just academic abilities, but a student’s mental and emotional abilities as well (Positive Action). These foundational thinkers established an approach that would become the core of social-emotional learning guidelines decades later, establishing something essential: academic achievement cannot be separated from emotional and social development.

1960s

Proof of a Concept

Before social-emotional learning was named a discipline, it was used as a proven intervention. In 1968, Dr. James Comer of Yale University’s Child Study Center began piloting what would become the Comer School Development Program in two of New Haven’s most underperforming schools serving predominantly Black student populations (Edutopia). His theory was to support the whole child in order to improve the educational experience of poor ethnic youth. Comer’s program focused on two poor, low-achieving elementary schools in New Haven, Connecticut; both schools had the lowest academic achievement and worst attendance in the city. In the program, the schools established a collaborative-management team consisting of teachers, parents, the principal, and a mental health worker to make decisions together on the schools’ academic and social programs. Their goal was to work together and adjust the ways in which schools were engaging with behavioral problems by better supporting the whole student (CASEL). The results were striking; by the early 1980s both schools had shown significant academic growth, exceeding the national averages. In addition, both schools saw a decline in truancy and behavior problems (CASEL). This change sparked a momentum to the social-emotional learning movement across the nation.

1980s

A Push for More

After seeing profound results from Dr. Comer’s program, the superintendent of the district in which the pilot schools resided called for a focus on social development of students. This birthed the New Haven Social Development Program that pioneered social-emotional learning strategies in K-12 classrooms. Around the same time, educator and researcher Dr. Weissberg and Dr. Elias chaired the Grant Consortium on the School-Based Promotion of Social Competence, bringing leaders together to build a framework for social-emotional learning in schools (CASEL). Finally, in 1989 the term “emotional intelligence” was coined by psychologists Peter Salovey and John D. Mayer, using it to describe the ability to monitor the needs of oneself and others, and use them to guide behaviors (CASEL).

1994

The Birth of Social-Emotional Learning as a Field

In 1994, a group of researchers, educators, child advocates, and practitioners with expertise across fields in emotional intelligence, child development, public health, prevention science, and more, came together to address the “missing piece” in education. (CASEL). They shared common ground that schools must attend to all needs of a child, including social and emotional needs. From this meeting, the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) began from this small conference in Kalamazoo, Michigan. This meeting also gave rise to the term “social and emotional learning” (CASEL). The first foundational contribution of CASEL was the CASEL 5, a framework of five broad, interrelated areas that would define the industry for decades to come. The CASEL 5, defined as self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making, established a universal language for SEL and remains the framework used today (CASEL).

Late 1990s

Social-Emotional Learning Goes Mainstream

In 1995, American psychologist, Daniel Goleman released the book, Emotional Intelligence: Why it Matters More Than IQ in which he detailed the concept of emotional intelligence. This was a cultural catalyst, as it was the first time that the concept of social-emotional learning was popularized in the public sphere (Positive Action). In his book, Goleman emphasized the importance of social skills and personal character, as well as the fact that these attributes can be taught (Positive Action). The book became a bestseller and made the case to parents, policymakers, and educators that emotional intelligence needed to be seen as more than a soft skill. With this book, Goleman created a public appetite to drive investment and policy action in social-emotional learning for decades to come (Positive Action).

2015

First Legitimization by Federal Policy

While widely supported across the globe, social-emotional learning did not see true federal action and support until the passing of the Every Student Succeeds Act, a reform of No Child Left Behind (Positive Action). In the Every Student Succeeds Act, the federal government authorized K-12 policy in support of higher quality K-12 education. Although the phrase “social and emotional learning” did not explicitly appear in the legislation, ESSA created multiple funding pathways for social-emotional learning programming in schools (RAND). School leaders were now able to use funding for nonacademic indicators of success, supporting student mental and behavioral health, as well as hiring support personnel to develop school climate and safety (Positive Action). This was the first time government money institutionalized social-emotional learning as a school priority.

2015-2020s

Smartphones and Technological Rise

Social media, the rise of AI, and advancements in digital technology dramatically changed education through the 2000s, and Social-Emotional Learning was not immune to this change. With smartphones landing in the hands of younger generations, educators saw dramatic changes in how students communicate, treat others, how isolated they feel, and how they perceive themselves (GovTech). According to a national survey carried out by EdWeek Research Center, 595 teachers, school leaders, and district leaders felt concerned about K-12 students being constantly online (GovTech). As technology continues to rise, schools have been searching for ways to engage students in Social-Emotional Learning through technology. Instead of trying to simply remove technology from students, educators worked toward “helping students learn to use social media in ways that support their mental health and well-being” (GovTech).

2020-Present

A Multi-Billion Dollar Industry is Born

The global pandemic of 2020 brought elevated attention to social-emotional learning as an industry. With students at home, the nation saw elevated levels of youth mental health struggles and educators worked to combat this trend (Positive Action). A cumulative effect of decades of research, advocacy, policy, and now cultural urgency, social-emotional learning has produced one of the fastest growing sectors of EdTech (IMARC Group). In 2021, Weissberg launched a Scholars Program to inspire the next generation of social-emotional learning scholars (Positive Action). This program was infused with money from a $123 billion investment to social-emotional learning which was presented in the American Rescue Plan Act (Positive Action). With this unprecedented investment, social-emotional learning as an industry skyrocketed. Today, research shows that educators are pushing for schools to deliver an array of social-emotional learning tools. The global market reached $4.0 billion in 2024 and is projected to grow to $21.1 billion by 2033 (IMARC Group). Driven by AI and digital platforms, the EdTech market for social-emotional learning is on the rise (IMARC Group).
